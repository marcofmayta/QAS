# QAS · Quechua Aimara Space

Learn about space through simple explanations and activities. QAS connects astronomy with the languages of communities in Peru.

## Stars and galaxies

Stars are huge spheres of hot gas that produce energy inside them. Our Sun is one of them. Light from other stars travels for years before reaching our eyes: looking into space also means looking into the past.

Stars gather in galaxies. We live in the Milky Way, home to billions of stars. On a dark night, part of it appears as a faint band across the sky.

Nebulae are clouds of gas and dust. Some form new stars; others contain material released by dying stars. Telescope photographs show details and colors that our eyes cannot always distinguish.

### An activity to share

Try this: draw two groups of stars and observe them again an hour later. Have they moved? Earth’s rotation makes them appear to move across the sky.

## The solar system

The solar system consists of the Sun and the bodies orbiting it. The Sun is a star; planets shine mainly by reflecting its light.

Moving outward from the Sun, the eight planets are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. The first four are rocky planets. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants; Uranus and Neptune are ice giants.

Saturn’s rings contain countless pieces of ice and rock. They may look solid in illustrations, but they consist of particles of different sizes.

There are also moons, asteroids, comets, and dwarf planets such as Pluto. Our Moon reflects sunlight. Its phases change with the relative positions of Earth, the Moon, and the Sun.

### An activity to share

Activity: draw the Moon over several nights and record each date. Never look directly at the Sun or view it through binoculars or telescopes without suitable solar filters.

## Languages and the sky

Inti and Willka are words associated with the Sun in Quechua and Aymara. Killa and Phaxsi name the Moon; Qoyllur and Wara wara name stars. Spellings and usage may vary across communities.

QAS brings astronomy and Indigenous languages together. There is no single Andean perspective: communities have their own voices, experiences, and ways of sharing knowledge.

This version includes articles in four languages. The Quechua and Aymara translations are drafts generated with AI assistance. They still need review and adaptation by community speakers.

### An activity to share

Try this together: ask a family member which words they use for the sky. With their permission, record the word, meaning, and community or language variety. Do not publish stories or recordings without permission.

## The Sun: our star

The Sun is the closest star to Earth. Its light and heat provide many of the conditions that life on our planet needs.

The Sun is made mostly of hydrogen and helium. In its core, nuclear fusion turns hydrogen into helium and releases energy. It is not a bonfire: its energy is produced in a different way.

Sunlight takes about eight minutes to reach Earth. This light also illuminates the Moon and the planets.

Never look directly at the Sun. Ordinary sunglasses do not protect your eyes for solar viewing. This activity involves looking at shadows without looking toward the Sun.

### An activity to share

Place an object on the ground in a safe location. Draw its shadow in the morning and afternoon. Record the time and compare the direction and size of the shadows.

- [NASA · Sun facts](https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/)

## The Moon and its phases

The Moon is Earth’s natural satellite. A natural satellite is a body that travels around a planet. The Moon reflects sunlight.

Half of the Moon receives sunlight. From Earth, we see different portions of that illuminated half. This makes the Moon appear to change shape. These changes are called phases.

At new moon, the illuminated side faces mainly away from Earth. At full moon, we see almost all of that illuminated side. Waxing and waning phases occur between them. The cycle lasts about 29 and a half days.

Earth’s shadow does not cause the phases. Earth’s shadow can darken the Moon during a lunar eclipse. The Moon can also be visible during the day, depending on its phase and position.

### An activity to share

Observe the Moon for a week whenever it is visible. Draw its illuminated part and record the date and time. If you cannot see it, note whether clouds or buildings blocked that direction.

## Earth, day, and the seasons

Earth is the planet where we live. It has an atmosphere, a layer of gases surrounding the planet, and liquid water on its surface.

Earth spins on its axis. This motion is called rotation and takes about 24 hours. The side facing the Sun has daytime. The opposite side has nighttime.

Earth also moves around the Sun. One complete orbit takes about one year. This motion is called revolution.

Earth’s axis is tilted. This tilt changes how much light each hemisphere receives during the year and causes the seasons. The seasons are not mainly caused by the distance from the Sun.

Puno is in the Southern Hemisphere. Changes in rainfall and temperature also depend on a community’s location and altitude. To discuss local weather patterns, observe and consult records from the area.

### An activity to share

Use a ball and a flashlight in a dark room. The ball represents Earth and the flashlight represents the Sun. Turn the ball and observe how its illuminated part changes.

## How to start observing the sky

Choose a familiar, safe location. Avoid roads, slopes, and unprotected shores. Children should go with an adult. Bring warm clothing and agree on a time to return.

Choose a night with few clouds and a place with little artificial light. Let your eyes adjust to the dark. You can start with the Moon and the brightest stars without using a telescope.

Record the date, time, location, and sky conditions. Draw the lights you observe. If you do not recognize an object, describe what you see before trying to name it.

Visible objects change with the time, date, and location. This page does not show a live sky map. Not all lights are stars: you may also observe planets, aircraft, or artificial satellites.

Comparing observations helps you learn. Keep drawings from different nights. They do not need to be perfect: they should help you remember what you saw.

### An activity to share

Download the observing guide at the bottom of the page. Complete one record and compare it with another person’s record. What did you both observe?

## Words for learning astronomy

Astronomy studies bodies in space and phenomena in the universe. This glossary explains words used in QAS articles.

These definitions describe scientific concepts. When translating them, a short explanation can be used if the community has no commonly used equivalent word.

**Star**: A body of hot gas that produces energy inside it. The Sun is a star.

**Planet**: In the solar system, a nearly spherical body that orbits the Sun, is not a moon, and gravitationally dominates its orbital neighborhood.

**Orbit**: The path of a body around another under the influence of gravity.

**Gravity**: Attraction between bodies that have mass. It keeps planets in orbit around the Sun.

**Natural satellite**: A natural body that orbits a planet or another larger body. The Moon orbits Earth.

**Galaxy**: A collection of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter held together by gravity.

**Constellation**: A defined region of the sky. It is often associated with an imagined pattern of stars that may be far apart from each other.

**Light-year**: The distance light travels in one year. It is a unit of distance, not time.

**Nebula**: A cloud of gas and dust in space. Stars form in some nebulae.

**Atmosphere**: A layer of gases surrounding a body, such as Earth.

### An activity to share

Choose three words. Explain each in your own words and add a drawing.

## An activity for school

Starting question: why does the illuminated portion of the Moon that we see change? This activity uses a model. A model represents some features of a phenomenon but does not reproduce every detail.

Materials: a flashlight, a light-colored ball, and a space where the lights can be dimmed. The flashlight represents the Sun. The ball represents the Moon. The observer represents a viewpoint from Earth.

Keep the flashlight still. Another person slowly moves the ball around the observer. Hold the ball slightly above head level so the body does not block the light. Do not shine the flashlight into anyone’s eyes.

Observe which part of the ball receives light and how much of that illuminated part you can see. Draw three positions. Discuss the difference between the illuminated part and the visible part.

The model does not represent real distances or sizes. It also cannot explain eclipse dates by itself. Its purpose is to show how our view of an illuminated sphere changes.

### An activity to share

Each participant explains one drawing in their preferred language. Record any remaining questions to investigate in another session.
